14 Best Things to Do in Cambridge

Cambridge is 31 colleges along one river, 50 minutes from King's Cross, and flat enough that everyone cycles. The Backs, where the college lawns run down to the Cam, are the reason to come, and the river is the way to see them; the colleges themselves open on their own schedules and shut for exams in May and early June. The River and the Backs

1. Punting the Backs

River Essential

The 45-minute stretch from Silver Street to Magdalene Bridge passes seven colleges from the water: Queens' and the Mathematical Bridge, King's Chapel across its lawn, Clare, Trinity, St John's and the Bridge of Sighs. Chauffeured punts have a student guide and run every few minutes from Mill Lane and Quayside; self-hire is cheaper and the Cam here is slow and shallow. Go early morning or after 5pm to have the river to yourself.

2. King's College Chapel

Chapel 1446–1515

Begun by Henry VI and finished by Henry VIII, with the largest fan-vaulted ceiling in the world and 26 stained-glass windows that survived the Civil War because Cromwell's troops were billeted inside. Rubens's Adoration of the Magi is behind the altar. Evensong is sung by the choir most days in term at 5.30pm and is free; the Christmas Eve service is the one broadcast worldwide. Daytime entry is ticketed and includes the grounds.

3. A walking tour with a graduate guide

Guided 2 hrs

The colleges charge separately and open erratically, so a two-hour walk with someone who studied here is the efficient introduction: the Senate House, King's Parade, Trinity's Great Court, St John's and the Bridge of Sighs, with the stories about Newton, Byron's bear and the night climbers. Most include optional entry to King's Chapel; the combined versions add a chauffeured punt afterwards.

4. The Mathematical Bridge

Bridge Queens' College

A wooden footbridge over the Cam at Queens' College, built in 1749 to a design by William Etheridge. The legend says Newton built it without nails and students who dismantled it could not reassemble it; Newton died 22 years before it was built and it has always had bolts. It is visible for free from Silver Street bridge and up close from inside Queens'.

5. Trinity College and the Wren Library

College Free library

The richest college, founded by Henry VIII in 1546, with the largest court in Oxford or Cambridge; Great Court is where the race against the clock in Chariots of Fire happens (filmed at Eton). The Wren Library, open free for two hours on weekday afternoons, holds Newton's own annotated Principia, the manuscript of Winnie-the-Pooh, and Wittgenstein's notebooks. The apple tree outside the gate is a graft from the one at Newton's family home.

Museums and Gardens

6. The Fitzwilliam Museum

Museum Free

The university's art museum, in a neoclassical building on Trumpington Street from 1848, with Titian, Monet, Picasso, Egyptian coffins, Greek vases and a collection of illuminated manuscripts. It is free and rarely busy. In 2006 a visitor tripped on a shoelace and smashed three Qing vases on the staircase; they were repaired and put back behind glass.

7. Cambridge University Botanic Garden

Garden 40 acres

Laid out in 1846 by John Henslow, Darwin's teacher, as a teaching collection, with 8,000 species, a glasshouse range, a winter garden planted for colour from December to March, and a rock garden. It is a 10-minute walk south of the centre by the station. The giant Amazon water lily flowers in the tropical house in late summer.

8. The Polar Museum

Museum Free

Part of the Scott Polar Research Institute on Lensfield Road, founded in 1920 as a memorial to Scott's expedition. The last letters Scott wrote in the tent are here, with Oates's sleeping bag, the sledging flags, and Inuit art. Small, free, and 20 minutes is enough. The institute still runs polar research upstairs.

9. The Round Church

Church 1130

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre, one of four surviving medieval round churches in England, built by the Fraternity of the Holy Sepulchre around 1130 in imitation of the one in Jerusalem. It is on Bridge Street by Magdalene Bridge and takes ten minutes. A short film inside explains the university's origins for visitors who have not had the tour.

Pubs, Walks and After Dark

10. The Eagle

Pub DNA

On Bene't Street, where Crick and Watson announced on 28 February 1953 that they had found the structure of DNA; the plaque is by the door and the pub pours an Eagle's DNA ale. The RAF Bar at the back has the ceiling burned with airmen's names and squadron numbers from 1940 to 1945, done with lighters and candles. Corpus Christi's Chronophage clock, with its grasshopper eating time, is 50 yards away.

11. Grantchester Meadows and the Orchard

Walk 3 miles

Walk or punt upstream from the centre through water meadows to Grantchester village, 3 miles, which has more Nobel laureates per head than anywhere else and the Orchard Tea Garden, serving tea under apple trees since 1897 to Rupert Brooke, Virginia Woolf, Keynes and Wittgenstein. Byron's Pool, where he swam, is just beyond. Return along the other bank or by bus.

12. The Cambridge ghost tour

Evening Guided

After dark the colleges empty and the alleys are the point. The guided ghost walks cover the headless horseman of Peterhouse, the Grey Lady of Merton Hall, and the college where a tutor's portrait is turned to the wall; they also cover the night climbers who put a car on the Senate House roof in 1958. Runs from the centre most evenings; 90 minutes.

13. Kettle's Yard

House Free

Four cottages on Castle Street knocked into one by Jim Ede, a Tate curator, in 1957, and left as a house rather than a gallery: Ben Nicholson, Miró, Brancusi and Gaudier-Brzeska sit among pebbles arranged in spirals on tables and plants on the windowsills. You ring the bell to be let in. Walk up Castle Mound next door for the only view over the city.

14. Ely Cathedral

Day trip 15 min

Fifteen minutes by train from Cambridge, Ely is a small city on what was an island in the fens, with a cathedral whose 14th-century Octagon lantern tower is unique in Europe: 400 tons of oak and lead hanging over the crossing. The Octagon tour climbs into the roof. Oliver Cromwell's house is opposite the cathedral and is the tourist office. The riverside walk along the Great Ouse is the afternoon.

FAQ: Cambridge

Is Cambridge worth a day trip from London?

Yes. Trains from King's Cross take 50 minutes and Liverpool Street about 75; the centre is a 20-minute walk or short bus from the station. A full day covers a walking tour, King's Chapel, a punt and the Fitzwilliam.

Which Cambridge colleges can you visit?

King's, Trinity, St John's, Queens' and Clare open to visitors most afternoons outside exam season (mid-May to mid-June) for a small fee each. Trinity's Wren Library is free on weekday afternoons. A guided tour includes King's entry and gets into courts that are otherwise closed.

Should I punt myself or take a chauffeured punt?

First-timers should take a chauffeured punt: a student guide names the colleges as you pass, and you see the Backs rather than the pole. Self-hire is half the price and fine if you have punted before; the Cam is shallow and slow.

Cambridge or Oxford?

Cambridge is smaller, flatter, and prettier from the river, with the better punting and King's Chapel. Oxford has more beyond the colleges and the Harry Potter film locations. Both are an hour from London; doing both in one day is possible but rushed.
