Glastonbury Tor & the Isle of Avalon

The hill that was once an island, and the legend that has never entirely left it

From almost anywhere on the flat Somerset Levels, Glastonbury Tor is unmissable: a steep, conical hill rising from the plain with a solitary medieval tower on its summit, looking slightly too dramatic to be real. On mornings when low cloud sits over the surrounding farmland, the Tor can appear to be floating above the mist — and it's easy to see why medieval people believed this place was magical.

Before drainage transformed the Somerset Levels, much of this landscape flooded seasonally. Glastonbury and the higher ground around it were genuinely islands for much of the year, surrounded by shallow water and reed beds. The landscape matched Arthurian descriptions of Avalon closely enough that the connection became fixed in medieval imagination and has never been entirely dislodged.

The Tor

The climb and the view

The Tor rises around 158 metres above sea level. The path from Glastonbury town winds up through terraced slopes — possibly ancient agricultural terraces or ritual pathways, though their original purpose is uncertain. The climb takes 20–30 minutes and is steep but manageable. At the top stands St Michael's Tower, the surviving remains of a medieval church largely destroyed by an earthquake in 1275. The views from the summit are extensive: across the Somerset Levels to the Bristol Channel, the Mendip Hills, the Vale of Avalon, and on clear days towards Wales.

Free · National Trust · Open at all times

The Avalon Legend

Where Arthur was taken — and supposedly found

In Arthurian legend, Avalon is a mystical island where mortally wounded King Arthur was carried after his final battle of Camlann. The identification of Glastonbury with Avalon developed in the medieval period, strengthened in 1191 when monks at Glastonbury Abbey claimed to have discovered a grave inscribed with the words "Here lies buried the famous King Arthur with Guinevere his second wife in the Isle of Avalon." Most historians regard this discovery as a medieval fabrication — conveniently timed to attract pilgrims and rebuild abbey finances after a catastrophic fire. The claimed remains no longer exist. But the legend the monks nurtured has outlasted their opportunism by eight centuries.

Glastonbury Abbey: ~£10 adults · Adjacent to town centre
Timing: Glastonbury Tor at sunset or at dusk is one of the finest sights in Somerset. If you're driving, allow time to stay for the evening light across the Levels. The Tor is silhouetted against the western sky at dusk in a way that genuinely justifies the mystical reputation.

The Chalice Well

A sacred spring at the foot of the Tor

The Chalice Well is a natural spring that has flowed continuously for at least 2,000 years, producing iron-rich red water. It has been considered sacred across different belief systems for much of that time. The well and its gardens are managed as a place of peace and reflection — the garden design is formal and beautiful. The legend connecting it to the Holy Grail (supposedly hidden here by Joseph of Arimathea) is entirely medieval invention, but the spring and its setting are genuinely atmospheric.

~£7 adults · At the foot of the Tor
