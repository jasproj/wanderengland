Stonehenge & the Summer Solstice

England's most ancient landmark — 5,000 years of mystery on Salisbury Plain

Nothing in England is older or more debated than Stonehenge. Built in phases between roughly 3000 and 2000 BCE, the stone circle on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire was already an ancient monument when the Romans arrived. Nobody knows exactly what it was for. That uncertainty is part of what makes it extraordinary.

It draws around a million visitors a year — including tens of thousands who come specifically for the summer solstice sunrise, one of the most atmospheric events on the English calendar.

The Stones: Scale and Construction

Bigger than they look in photographs

The tallest standing sarsen stones rise roughly 9 metres above ground — about the height of a three-storey building — with a further 2.4 metres buried below to anchor them. Each weighs up to 25 tonnes. The bluestones in the inner ring, smaller but still substantial, were transported from the Preseli Hills in Wales, around 200 miles away. How exactly they were moved remains a subject of active archaeological research. The current best understanding involves timber sledges, rollers, and waterways — but the logistics were staggering for any period, let alone 3000 BCE.

UNESCO World Heritage Site · Managed by English Heritage

The Solstice Alignment

The sun rises exactly where it should, 5,000 years later

On the summer solstice (around 21 June), the rising sun appears directly behind the Heel Stone — a single rough sarsen standing outside the main circle — and its light shines along the monument's central axis into the heart of the circle. The same axis aligns with the winter solstice sunset. This alignment was clearly intentional, requiring sophisticated astronomical knowledge to plan and execute. Whether Stonehenge was a calendar, a temple, a burial site, or all three is still genuinely unknown.

Summer solstice open access — register in advance at English Heritage
Solstice tip: For the summer solstice open-access event, tens of thousands attend and the atmosphere is remarkable. English Heritage usually opens the site from around 7pm the evening before through mid-morning on solstice day. Bring layers — it's an open plain and June dawns can be cold.

The Standard Visit

What to expect on a timed-ticket day visit

Standard visits follow a path around the outside of the stone circle — you cannot enter or touch the stones on a regular ticket. A modern visitor centre includes a permanent exhibition covering the monument's history, a full-scale replica of a Neolithic house, and reproductions of objects found at the site. Audio guides are included. The walk from the visitor centre to the stones is about 1.5km and takes 20–30 minutes each way. Allow at least 2 hours for a satisfying visit.

~£26 adults (2026) · Book timed tickets in advance

Getting There

Stonehenge sits near Amesbury in Wiltshire, roughly 90 miles from London. The most practical approach from London is a guided day trip, which typically includes transport plus sometimes Bath or Salisbury Cathedral. By train, the closest station is Salisbury (around 90 minutes from London Waterloo), with the Stonehenge Tour bus connecting from there. Driving is easy — there's a large car park at the visitor centre.

Combination tip: Stonehenge and Bath make a natural same-day pairing — they're only 40 miles apart. Many guided tours run this route.
