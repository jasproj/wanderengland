15 Best Things to Do in the Cotswolds

The Cotswolds is an 800-square-mile stretch of limestone hills between Oxford and Bath, with villages built from the same honey-coloured stone they sit on. There is no single town to base yourself in and no train line through the middle, so the trick is picking a cluster of villages and doing them properly rather than ticking off a dozen from a coach window. This list runs north to south, with the walks, houses and gardens that justify the detour. Tours you can book here →

The Villages

1. Bourton-on-the-Water

Village Busy

The River Windrush runs down the middle of the high street under five low stone footbridges, which is why every coach stops here. Arrive before 10am or after 4pm and it is a different place. The Model Village is a one-ninth scale copy of Bourton itself, built in 1937 and including a model of the model. Birdland, on the edge of the village, keeps the only king penguins in England.

2. Bibury and Arlington Row

Village Photo stop

Arlington Row is a terrace of 14th-century weavers' cottages beside the River Coln, owned by the National Trust and lived in by tenants, so stay on the lane. The Bibury Trout Farm next door has been working since 1902 and sells smoked trout at the gate. Most people see the row and leave; the circular walk up to Bibury Court and back through the water meadows takes forty minutes and loses the crowd entirely.

3. Stow-on-the-Wold

Market town Antiques

The highest town in the Cotswolds, at 800 feet, with a huge market square that once held sheep fairs of 20,000 animals. The north door of St Edward's church is framed by two yew trees that have grown into the stonework, and is the most photographed door in the county. Stow is the best antiques town in the hills, with a dozen dealers around the square, and the Porch House claims to be England's oldest inn (947 AD).

4. Castle Combe

Village Film set

Officially in Wiltshire but on every Cotswolds itinerary, Castle Combe has no visible modern additions: no streetlights, no TV aerials, and a 14th-century market cross in the middle. Steven Spielberg filmed War Horse here and Doctor Dolittle (1967) before that. It is a single street, so twenty minutes covers it; combine it with the Castle Combe racing circuit on the hill above if there is a track day on.

5. Chipping Campden and the Cotswold Way

Walk Start point

The 102-mile Cotswold Way starts (or ends) at the 17th-century Market Hall in Chipping Campden and runs along the escarpment to Bath. The first stage to Broadway is 6 miles over Dover's Hill, where the Cotswold Olimpick Games have been held since 1612, shin-kicking included. The town itself is the best-preserved wool town in the region; Grevel House on the High Street dates from 1380.

Houses, Gardens and Views

6. Broadway Tower

Viewpoint 1,024 ft

A 65-foot folly built in 1798 on the second-highest point in the Cotswolds. On a clear day the view takes in 16 counties, and you can pick out the Welsh mountains and the Malverns. The tower was a retreat for William Morris and the Pre-Raphaelites; the top floor is a small museum about them. There is a nuclear bunker in the grounds, open at weekends, from when the site was a Royal Observer Corps post.

7. Sudeley Castle

Castle Gardens

The only private castle in England with a queen buried in its grounds: Katherine Parr, Henry VIII's last wife, lies in St Mary's chapel. The ruined Tithe Barn is overgrown with roses, and the Queens' Garden is laid out in Tudor style with 80 varieties. The castle is a mile outside Winchcombe and is still a family home, so some rooms are closed on weekdays. Allow three hours with the gardens.

8. Hidcote Manor Garden

Garden National Trust

The most influential garden of the 20th century in England, laid out from 1907 by Lawrence Johnston as a series of outdoor rooms divided by yew and hornbeam hedges. The Red Border and the Long Walk are the famous bits. Kiftsgate Court, directly across the lane, is a privately owned garden with a 60-foot-tall Kiftsgate rose; buy the joint ticket and do both in an afternoon.

9. Blenheim Palace

Palace UNESCO

Technically Oxfordshire, but 10 miles from the Cotswold edge and on most tour routes. Winston Churchill was born here in 1874; the room is on the ground floor near the entrance. The 2,000-acre Capability Brown park is bigger than the palace deserves attention for, and the formal Water Terraces are the best spot. A standard ticket converts to a free annual pass if you ask at the gate.

10. Snowshill Manor

House Eccentric

A Tudor manor filled with 22,000 objects collected by Charles Paget Wade between 1919 and 1951: Samurai armour, bicycles, musical instruments, Balinese masks. Wade lived in the cottage next door because the house had no room left. It is the oddest house the National Trust owns and the one children remember. The village above it is tiny and is where the lavender fields are, in bloom late June to early August.

Getting Out

11. Quad biking at Notgrove

Active Book ahead

Notgrove Estate, between Bourton and Cheltenham, runs guided quad-bike trails through 1,500 acres of farmland and woodland on the top of the wolds. The Taster is an hour and suits first-timers; longer routes climb to the ridge for the view over the Windrush valley. Helmets and overalls are provided; you will still get muddy from October to April.

Bookable: Quad biking: The Taster at Notgrove (QuadQuest Ltd. · from £76) — live availability on FareHarbor.

12. Boats on the upper Thames at Lechlade

River Summer

Lechlade is where the Thames becomes navigable, and the river here is a quiet green channel between water meadows rather than the London version. You can hire pedalos and canoes by the hour at the Riverside, or a day cruiser from Buscot to potter the 4 miles down to Radcot Bridge, the oldest bridge on the river. St John's Lock, the first on the Thames, has the statue of Old Father Thames.

Bookable: Day cruiser hire on the upper Thames at Buscot (Cotswold Boat Hire · from £159) — live availability on FareHarbor.

13. Butter churning and the Broadway Museum

Hands-on Rainy day

The Broadway Museum and Art Gallery is a 17th-century coaching inn on the high street with a changing exhibition from the Ashmolean upstairs. Its workshop programme is the reason to go: the butter-churning session uses a traditional plunge churn and sends you home with what you made. Broadway itself has the widest high street in the Cotswolds and the Gordon Russell furniture museum at the far end.

Bookable: Butter churning workshop at the Broadway Museum (Broadway Museum & Art Gallery · from £47.7) — live availability on FareHarbor.

14. Cheltenham

Town Regency

The Cotswolds' big town, and the easiest base with a train station. The Promenade and Montpellier are Regency terraces built for spa visitors after 1788, and the Pittville Pump Room still serves the salty spa water. The racecourse hosts the Festival in March; the Literature Festival is in October and the Jazz Festival in late April. Cheltenham is a 20-minute drive from Winchcombe and 30 from Bourton.

15. A half-day tour from Bath

Guided Small group

If you are based in Bath, a half-day minibus tour reaches Castle Combe, Lacock (where Harry Potter and Pride and Prejudice were filmed) and the southern villages in four hours with cream tea included, which is the practical way to see them without a car. Full-day versions from Oxford and London reach Bourton, Bibury and Stow but spend half the day on the road.

FAQ: the Cotswolds

Which part of the Cotswolds is best to visit?

The north (Chipping Campden, Broadway, Stow, Bourton) has the classic villages and most of the day-tour routes. The south (Castle Combe, Lacock, Tetbury) is quieter and closer to Bath. If you have one day, pick one half; driving between them takes an hour each way.

Can you visit the Cotswolds without a car?

Yes, but it takes planning. Moreton-in-Marsh and Cheltenham have train stations; the Pulham's bus links Moreton, Stow and Bourton; and guided half-day or full-day tours run from Bath, Oxford and London. Bibury, Castle Combe and Snowshill are hard to reach on public transport.

How many days do you need in the Cotswolds?

Two days covers the northern villages and one or two gardens or houses at a comfortable pace. A day trip from London or Bath works for three or four villages. Walkers doing the whole Cotswold Way need 7 to 10 days.

When is the best time to visit the Cotswolds?

May and June for gardens and long days, late June to early August for the lavender at Snowshill, and September to October for fewer coaches and autumn colour at Batsford and Westonbirt arboretums. Villages like Bourton and Bibury are very busy on summer weekends; go early in the day.
