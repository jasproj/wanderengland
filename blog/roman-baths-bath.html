The Roman Baths at Bath

A sacred spring, a goddess, and 2,000 years of history beneath the Georgian city

Beneath the honey-coloured Georgian streetscape of Bath, the Romans built one of the most elaborate bathing complexes in their northern empire. The hot spring that drew them here — producing around a million litres of water per day at roughly 45°C — had already been considered sacred by the Celts for centuries before the first Roman soldier arrived. It still flows today, as it has for thousands of years.

The Roman Baths complex is one of the best-preserved Roman sites in northern Europe. Visiting feels less like a museum and more like looking through a window into a world that is both very distant and surprisingly recognisable.

The Sacred Spring

Where the Celts worshipped and the Romans built a temple

The spring at the heart of the complex produces hot, mineral-rich water from deep underground. The Celts worshipped the goddess Sulis here. When the Romans arrived in the 1st century CE, they fused Sulis with their own goddess Minerva to create Sulis Minerva and built a temple precinct around the spring. Excavations have recovered thousands of votive offerings thrown into the water — coins, carved gemstones, and over 130 curse tablets in which people asked the goddess to punish those who had wronged them. The tablets are among the most intimate surviving voices from Roman Britain.

Spring discovered in the 1870s · Museum holds thousands of artefacts

The Great Bath

The centrepiece — and it was roofed in Roman times

The Great Bath is an open-air lead-lined pool, roughly 25 metres long, filled with the natural thermal water which gives it a vivid green-blue colour (caused by algae in the light). In Roman times it was enclosed within a vast vaulted hall — the columns and terrace you see above it are Victorian additions. Statues of Roman emperors and governors line the terrace today. The actual Roman floor and lead lining are the originals, dating back nearly 2,000 years. Standing at the edge is genuinely striking.

Original lead lining from the 1st century CE · Pool not for swimming

The Museum

Thousands of objects found at the bottom of the spring

The museum adjoining the baths holds finds from decades of excavation. Highlights include the gilded bronze head of Sulis Minerva, the carved stone face known as the Gorgon's head from the temple pediment, and display cases of curse tablets with translations. The exhibits give real texture to the people who used this place — pilgrims, soldiers, merchants — and what they hoped, feared, and believed.

After the Baths: The Thermae Bath Spa

The Roman Baths themselves are a museum — you cannot bathe in them. But a five-minute walk away, the Thermae Bath Spa uses the same natural hot spring water in a modern facility with a rooftop pool overlooking Bath's rooftops. It's a worthwhile combination: history in the morning, a warm soak in the afternoon.

Practical note: Book Thermae Bath Spa in advance, especially for weekends. The open-air rooftop pool is the main draw and gets busy.
