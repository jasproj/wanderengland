18 Best Things to Do in Brighton, England

A Regency palace, a Victorian pier, a pebble beach, and the best independent shopping on the south coast — an hour from London

Brighton is where London goes to let its hair down. An hour by train from Victoria, it's a seaside city with an actual personality: a palace that looks like it wandered off from Jaipur, a pier full of arcade machines and doughnuts, a warren of lanes selling vintage clothes and antique jewellery, and a nightlife scene that doesn't quit. It's also the unofficial LGBTQ+ capital of the UK and home to more vegetarian restaurants per head than anywhere else in England.

It works as a day trip from London, but it works better with a night or two — the seafront at dusk, the pubs in North Laine, and a Seven Sisters cliff walk the next morning are the parts most day-trippers miss.

1. Royal Pavilion

George IV's seaside fantasy palace

Built for the Prince Regent between 1787 and 1823, the Pavilion is an Indian-style palace on the outside and a riot of Chinese-inspired decor inside — dragons, bamboo, and a banqueting room chandelier that weighs a ton. It's the single most photographed building in Brighton and the one attraction you shouldn't skip. The audio guide is included and worth using; allow 90 minutes. The surrounding gardens are free.

Adults about £21.50 · Allow 90 min
Tip: Book online for a timed slot — weekend afternoons sell out in summer. The Pavilion is a 10-minute walk from the station and sits right between the Lanes and the pier, so it's the natural first stop.

2. The Lanes

Brighton's medieval heart

A tangle of narrow alleys south of North Street, built on the footprint of the original fishing village. Today it's antique jewellers, independent boutiques, and some of the city's best small restaurants. Meeting House Lane and Dukes Lane are the prettiest stretches. Easy to get lost in, which is the point. The Cricketers on Black Lion Street claims to be Brighton's oldest pub (1547) and is a decent stop for a pint.

Free · Best 11am–5pm

3. North Laine

Where locals actually shop

Not to be confused with the Lanes. North Laine is the grid of streets between the station and the Pavilion — vintage clothing, record shops, vegan cafes, street art, and Snoopers Paradise, a two-floor flea market on Kensington Gardens that's a Brighton institution. On Saturdays, Upper Gardner Street hosts an open-air flea market. This is the neighborhood that gives Brighton its reputation.

Free · Saturday flea market

4. Brighton Palace Pier

The classic English seaside experience

Opened in 1899, still going. Arcade machines, a helter skelter, a log flume, fairground rides at the far end, and fish and chips or doughnuts in between. Entry is free (£1 at peak times); rides cost extra. It's touristy and slightly tatty and that's the appeal — a proper Victorian pleasure pier. Walk to the end for the best view back at the city and the seafront.

Free entry · Rides extra

5. Brighton i360

The seafront observation tower

A 162-metre pole with a glass pod that rises to 138 metres for a 25-minute "flight" with views along the coast to the South Downs and, on a clear day, the Isle of Wight. It closed in late 2024 and reopened in March 2025 under new ownership. Tickets start around £18.50 online. Worth it on a clear day; skip it if it's hazy — you're paying for the view. The base is a good place to see the ruins of the West Pier.

From £18.50 · 25-min flight

6. West Pier Ruins

Brighton's most photographed skeleton

The West Pier opened in 1866, closed in 1975, and burned in 2003. What's left is a rusting iron frame standing in the sea — and it's oddly beautiful, especially at sunset when starlings gather in murmurations around it (October to March). Best viewed from the beach near the i360. Free, obviously.

Free · Sunset · Starlings Oct–Mar

7. Brighton Beach & Seafront

Pebbles, beach huts, and a two-mile promenade

The beach is shingle, not sand — bring sandals. But the seafront is the main event: a wide promenade from the Marina in the east to Hove Lawns in the west, with bars, seafood stalls, and the Artists' Quarter under the arches near the pier. Hove's pastel beach huts are 20 minutes' walk west. Swimming is fine in summer (water is cold), and there's a sea-swimming community year-round.

Free · Bring sandals
Tip: Marrocco's on Hove seafront makes the best ice cream in the city — the pistachio is the one to get. It's a 25-minute walk west from the pier, and the walk along the front is the point.

8. Sea Life Brighton

The world's oldest operating aquarium

Opened in 1872 in Victorian vaulted arches under the seafront, right by the pier. Sharks, rays, turtles, and a glass-bottom boat ride over the main tank. Small compared to modern aquariums, but the Victorian architecture is the draw. Good for families and rainy days. Book online for cheaper tickets.

Adults about £20 online · 1–2 hours

9. Brighton Museum & Art Gallery

In the Pavilion gardens

A compact museum with a strong 20th-century design collection (Dali's Mae West Lips sofa lives here), fashion galleries, ancient Egypt, and a good section on Brighton's own history from fishing village to seaside boom. Right next to the Pavilion, so easy to combine.

Adults about £9 · 1 hour

10. Volk's Electric Railway

The oldest electric railway in the world

Running along the seafront since 1883, this narrow-gauge line trundles just over a mile from the pier to the Marina. It's slow, charming, and costs a few pounds. Take it east to Black Rock and walk back along the front, or continue to the Marina for a look at the boats. Runs April to September.

Seasonal · A few pounds

11. Brighton Marina

Boats, restaurants, and the Undercliff Walk

The marina itself is a modern development with chain restaurants and a cinema — nothing special. The reason to go is the Undercliff Walk, a paved path that runs three miles east from the marina along the base of the chalk cliffs to Saltdean. Flat, sea on one side, white cliffs on the other. Good for a cycle or an easy walk.

Free · Undercliff Walk 3 miles

12. Seven Sisters Cliffs

The best day trip from Brighton

A row of seven chalk cliffs rising from the sea, about 20 miles east of Brighton. The walk from Seaford across the Seven Sisters to Birling Gap and Beachy Head is one of the best coastal walks in England — about 8 miles, 4 hours, and a lot of up and down. Take the 12/12X bus from Brighton to Seaford (about 45 minutes) or to Exceat for the Seven Sisters Country Park. Birling Gap has a National Trust cafe and beach access.

Free · Bus 12/12X · Full day
Tip: The classic photo of the Seven Sisters is taken from Seaford Head, looking east across Cuckmere Haven. Get off the bus at Seaford, walk up onto Seaford Head, and you'll see it. Stay well back from the cliff edge — it erodes constantly and there are no barriers.

13. Devil's Dyke

The South Downs on Brighton's doorstep

A dramatic dry valley on the South Downs, 5 miles north of the city, with views Constable called "the grandest in the world." The 77 bus runs from Brighton Pier to the top (about 30 minutes, weekends and summer). Walk the ridge, watch the paragliders, then lunch at the Devil's Dyke pub. A half-day that gets you out of the city and onto the Downs.

Free · Bus 77 · Half day

14. Brighton Dome & the Music Scene

Gigs in the Pavilion's old stables

Brighton Dome — originally the Prince Regent's riding house — is the city's main concert hall, hosting everything from orchestras to touring bands. Beyond that, Brighton has a live music scene disproportionate to its size: Concorde 2 on the seafront, the Prince Albert (with its famous mural of dead musicians), the Hope & Ruin, and Green Door Store under the station arches. Check listings; there's something on most nights.

Ticketed · Evenings

15. Kemptown

The LGBTQ+ heart of Brighton

East of the pier, Kemptown is Brighton's gay village — bars along St James's Street, the beachfront clubs, and Brighton Pride in August, which is the biggest in the UK (the parade draws 300,000+). Outside Pride it's a relaxed neighborhood with good cafes, Regency squares, and a less touristy feel than the centre. The "Piers and Queers" walking tour covers the history.

Free · Pride in August

16. Eat Vegetarian

The most veggie-friendly city in England

Terre à Terre on East Street is the flagship — a vegetarian restaurant that's been winning awards since 1993 and where meat-eaters don't notice the absence. Food for Friends in the Lanes is the more affordable option. The Earth & Stars pub in North Laine is entirely vegan. Even the fish-and-chip shops do a good battered halloumi.

£15–50 per head

17. Fish and Chips on the Beach

Because you have to

Skip the pier stalls and go to a proper chippy: Bardsley's on Baker Street (North Laine, since 1926) or the Regency Restaurant on the seafront opposite the West Pier for sit-down with a view. Then eat on the beach and defend your chips from the gulls, which are enormous and fearless.

£10–15

18. A Night Out

Pubs, cocktails, and clubs

Start in North Laine — the Great Eastern for whisky and beer, the Basketmakers Arms for a classic pub with hidden notes in the tins on the walls. Move to the Lanes for cocktails (Plotting Parlour, the Black Dove in Kemptown). Then the seafront clubs under the arches — Coalition, Patterns, and the Arch — go late. Brighton's nightlife runs seven nights a week, and it's a big reason people come from London for the weekend.

Late · Every night

Practical Tips

Getting there. Train from London Victoria (Southern, about 60 minutes) or London Bridge / St Pancras / Blackfriars (Thameslink, 65–75 minutes). Trains run every 10–15 minutes. Off-peak advance returns start around £20–30. Brighton station is a 10-minute downhill walk to the seafront. Gatwick Airport is 30 minutes by train.

Getting around. Walk. The station, the Lanes, the Pavilion, the pier, and the beach are all within 15 minutes of each other. Buses (Brighton & Hove) cover Hove, the Marina, Devil's Dyke, and the Seven Sisters. Don't bring a car — parking is scarce and expensive.

When to visit. May to September for beach weather, with August the busiest (Pride, school holidays). May brings the Brighton Festival and Fringe — the biggest arts festival in England. Spring and autumn are quieter with decent weather. Winter is bleak but the starling murmurations over the West Pier (October–March) are a real draw.

Budget. A day trip from London runs roughly £60–100 per person including train, Pavilion entry, lunch, and a drink. Overnight, expect £100–200 for a decent hotel or B&B; seafront rooms cost more. Brighton is cheaper than London for food and drink but not by much.

Local tip: Avoid the seafront restaurants between the piers — tourist prices for mediocre food. Walk five minutes inland to the Lanes or North Laine and the quality jumps while the prices drop.
