Hadrian's Wall

The edge of the Roman world — 73 miles of ancient stone across the north of England

In 122 CE, Emperor Hadrian ordered the construction of a wall across the full width of Britain — from the Solway Firth on the west coast to the River Tyne in the east. It took at least six years to build and ran for 73 miles (80 Roman miles). For nearly 300 years it marked the north-west frontier of the Roman Empire: the point where the known world ended and the unconquered north began.

Most of it still exists. Walking sections of the wall today — especially where it follows the dramatic Whin Sill escarpment in Northumberland — is one of the most immersive experiences in England's landscape.

What the Wall Was For

Frontier, customs post, and statement of power

The traditional interpretation is that the wall was a defensive barrier against raids from unconquered tribes north of the frontier. More recent thinking holds that its primary purpose may have been control — regulating movement, collecting tolls, and projecting Roman authority. The wall was not simply a line of stone: it was supported by a series of forts, milecastles every Roman mile, and turrets every third of a mile, all garrisoned and interconnected. The ditch to the north and the Vallum earthwork to the south made the entire zone a controlled military corridor.

Construction began 122 CE · UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987

Housesteads Roman Fort

The best-preserved Roman fort in Britain

Housesteads (Vercovicium in Latin) sits on the highest point of the central section and commands views in both directions along the wall. The fort held around 800 auxiliary soldiers at its peak. The visible remains include the headquarters building, the commanding officer's house, barracks, granaries, and the hospital. The Roman latrines — a multi-seat communal facility with channels for water — are the most frequently visited. The English Heritage museum at the site gives excellent context. The walk from the car park up to the fort takes about 15 minutes along a rough track.

~£9 adults · Allow 2 hours including the walk
Best photo spot: Walk a quarter mile west of Housesteads to the section where the wall follows the Whin Sill ridge. The dolerite escarpment drops steeply to the north and the wall curves dramatically — it's the iconic Hadrian's Wall view.

Vindolanda: The Writing Tablets

The most remarkable Roman finds in Britain

Vindolanda is a Roman fort just south of the main wall, with excavations that have been running for decades. The site is particularly famous for the Vindolanda tablets — thin wooden writing tablets preserved in the waterlogged ground for nearly 2,000 years. They contain mundane but extraordinarily intimate messages: a birthday invitation from one officer's wife to another, a request for more socks and underwear (because northern Britain was cold), lists of provisions, and complaints from soldiers. Originals are in the British Museum; the Vindolanda museum has excellent replicas and explanations.

~£9 adults · Active excavations visible most of the year

Walking the Wall

The Hadrian's Wall Path is an 84-mile National Trail from Wallsend to Bowness-on-Solway. The most dramatic and best-preserved section — Steel Rigg to Housesteads — takes around 2–3 hours at a relaxed pace and is the highlight of the whole route. Good footwear and waterproofs are essential: this is exposed upland country and the weather changes quickly.
