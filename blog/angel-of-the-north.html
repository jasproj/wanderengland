The Angel of the North

20 metres tall, 54-metre wingspan, standing over a former coalfield since 1998 — England's most-seen modern sculpture

Every year, an estimated 33 million people see the Angel of the North. Most of them don't stop. Travelling down the A1 or on the East Coast Main Line, they see a rust-coloured steel figure on a hill, wings spread wide against the Gateshead sky, and then it's gone. For those who do stop, walking up to the base and standing beneath it is a different experience altogether — the scale is larger and the presence is more powerful than the motorway glimpse suggests.

Antony Gormley's sculpture was completed in February 1998. It stands 20 metres tall with a wingspan of 54 metres — roughly the span of a large passenger aircraft. It weighs 208 tonnes and is engineered to withstand wind speeds of over 100 miles per hour. Its site is a former colliery, and Gormley has spoken of it as a way of acknowledging the industrial history of the north-east — the coal and steel workers who shaped this landscape — while looking forward.

The Sculpture Up Close

Rust-coloured steel with visible ribs

The Angel is made from COR-TEN weathering steel, which has been allowed to oxidise, giving it the characteristic rust-brown colour. Vertical ribs run across the body and wings — not decoration but structural: they direct wind forces down into the foundations, which extend 20 metres into the ground to anchor the sculpture against the exposed hilltop winds. The body is based, like much of Gormley's figurative work, on a cast of his own body. The wings are angled slightly forward rather than horizontal, which some visitors describe as giving the figure a sheltering quality.

A Contested Start, a Beloved Outcome

How opposition became affection

The Angel of the North was controversial before it was built. Some local councillors objected; the local press ran critical stories; residents worried about traffic and the cost. Within a few years of installation, public opinion had shifted dramatically. The sculpture was adopted as a symbol of regional pride, appearing on merchandise, in advertising, and as a shorthand image for north-east England internationally. It now routinely appears in polls of favourite British artworks. The speed of the reversal — from objection to affection — is itself interesting, and is often cited in discussions of public art and community identity.

Commissioned by Gateshead Metropolitan Borough Council · Artist: Antony Gormley
Best light: Morning light coming from the east illuminates the eastern face of the Angel most directly. The sculpture on the horizon at dusk, from the A1 travelling north, with fading light behind it, is the view most people remember.

Gateshead Quays

The Angel stands about 4 miles south of the Gateshead Quays, where the Baltic Centre for Contemporary Art, the Sage Gateshead (one of England's best music venues), and the Gateshead Millennium Bridge — a tilting pedestrian bridge over the Tyne — cluster around the riverfront. Newcastle is across the river. Combining a stop at the Angel with a visit to the Quays and Newcastle makes a full and worthwhile day in the north-east.
