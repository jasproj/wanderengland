The Jurassic Coast & Durdle Door

185 million years of Earth history, one limestone arch, and the finest coastal walking in southern England

The Jurassic Coast runs for 96 miles along the shores of Dorset and Devon, from Exmouth in the west to Studland Bay in the east. In 2001 it became the first natural UNESCO World Heritage Site in England, recognised for the extraordinary geological record embedded in its cliffs — a continuous sequence of rock strata spanning roughly 185 million years, from the Triassic through the Jurassic and into the Cretaceous.

Its most photographed landmark is Durdle Door: a natural limestone arch near Lulworth that has become one of the defining images of the English coastline.

Durdle Door

The most photographed arch in England

Durdle Door is a natural arch formed by marine erosion eating through a band of harder Portland limestone, leaving the arch standing while the softer rock around it was worn away. The process began around 140 million years ago, though the arch in its current form is much more recent. It stands at the foot of steep chalk downland, accessible from a clifftop car park via a steep path. The beach below is shingle and sand — excellent in good weather, dramatic in any. Durdle Door is privately owned by the Lulworth Estate but open to visitors.

Car park charge applies · Steep descent to beach · No facilities on beach
Photo tip: The classic view of Durdle Door is from the cliff above the arch looking west — arrive early morning for the best light and fewest people. The arch also looks spectacular from the water, accessible by kayak or boat from Lulworth Cove.

Lulworth Cove

A nearly circular cove carved by the sea

Just east of Durdle Door, Lulworth Cove is one of the most perfect natural harbours in England — an almost circular bay formed by the sea breaking through a gap in the limestone to erode the softer rock behind it. The Heritage Centre at the top of the beach explains the geology clearly. From the cove, a 15-minute walk over the chalk ridge leads to Durdle Door, making the two an obvious pair.

Village with cafés and car park · Heritage Centre free

Fossil Hunting at Charmouth and Lyme Regis

The world's Mary Anning found ichthyosaurs here

At the western end of the Jurassic Coast, the beaches around Charmouth and Lyme Regis are among the richest fossil locations in the world. Mary Anning, a local fossil hunter working in the early 19th century, discovered the first complete ichthyosaur skeleton here. The cliffs continuously erode, releasing new material, and the beach shingle after storms can yield ammonites, belemnites, and other Jurassic sea creatures. The Lyme Regis Museum dedicates significant space to Anning's story. Guided fossil walks with local experts are the most productive approach for finding specimens.

Guided fossil walks from ~£10 · Best after storms · Never hammer cliffs
