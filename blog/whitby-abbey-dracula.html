Whitby Abbey & the Dracula Connection

Gothic ruins, 199 steps, a churchyard above the sea — the town that gave Bram Stoker his masterpiece

Whitby is built on two cliffs facing each other across a harbour mouth, with the River Esk running through the gap between them. The lower town has fish quays, jet jewellery shops, and bakeries selling the local speciality, Whitby gingerbread. Above the east cliff, reached by 199 stone steps, the ruins of Whitby Abbey stand against the sky alongside the ancient churchyard of St Mary's. It is one of the most atmospheric settings in England.

In the summer of 1890, Bram Stoker arrived here on holiday. He already had the outline of a vampire story. What Whitby gave him was the setting that made it unforgettable.

Whitby Abbey

1,400 years of history on a clifftop

The abbey was founded in 657 CE by Abbess Hild, one of the most important figures in early English Christianity. In 664 the Synod of Whitby was held here, resolving the fundamental question of whether the English church would follow Roman or Celtic Christian practice — a decision with lasting consequences for Christianity in Britain. The abbey was destroyed by Viking raids in the 9th century, rebuilt by Norman monks from 1078, and dissolved by Henry VIII in 1539. The standing ruins date from the 13th and 14th centuries. Their setting — perched directly above the cliff edge with the North Sea visible through the empty windows — is extraordinary.

~£10 adults · English Heritage · Allow 1–1.5 hours

The Dracula Connection

Where Stoker found his name and his setting

Bram Stoker arrived at Whitby in late July 1890, staying at 6 Royal Crescent. He explored the town, climbed the steps to the churchyard, and visited Whitby library — where he found the name "Dracula" in a book about Wallachian history. The novel published in 1897 places key scenes in Whitby: the Count's ship runs aground in the harbour during a storm, a large dog leaps from the hull and races up the cliff, and Lucy Westenra is found drained of blood on a bench in St Mary's churchyard. The abbey, the steps, the churchyard, and the harbour all appear recognisably in the text.

Stoker's bench in St Mary's churchyard · Whitby Museum has Dracula exhibits
Best time to visit: Whitby Goth Weekend (April and October) transforms the town with thousands of visitors in Victorian and Gothic costumes. Even outside Goth Weekend, the town has significant character — the narrow streets of the old town, the smoky kippers from Fortunes Smokehouse, and the sea views reward a longer stay.

St Mary's Churchyard

The bench where Lucy was bitten

St Mary's Church sits next to the abbey ruins, with its churchyard spreading across the clifftop and benches facing the harbour and the North Sea. The headstones, many worn smooth by centuries of wind and salt, include sailors, whalers, and fishermen. The view from the cliff edge — over the red rooftops of Whitby below, the harbour mouth, and the open sea beyond — is memorable in any weather. In fog, with the abbey ruins behind you, the atmosphere is precisely what Stoker was after.
