The White Cliffs of Dover

England's most symbolic natural landmark — chalk walls rising from the Channel, visible from France

For most of human history, the White Cliffs of Dover were the first sight of England for anyone crossing the Channel from the continent. Romans, Normans, medieval merchants, soldiers returning from wars in France — all saw the same thing: a long white wall of chalk rising from the sea, stark and unmistakable. The symbolism attached to this view is enormous, and standing on the clifftops looking back out across the Channel, you feel the weight of it.

The cliffs run for about 8 miles on each side of Dover. At their highest near the town, they reach around 350 feet (roughly 110 metres) above sea level. They are composed of chalk — almost pure calcium carbonate laid down as the compressed remains of microscopic sea creatures during the Late Cretaceous period, roughly 70–100 million years ago.

The Clifftop Walk

The National Trust stretch: the classic view

The National Trust manages the most accessible section of the cliffs, starting from a visitor centre about 2 miles east of Dover town centre. From the visitor centre, a well-maintained path follows the clifftop past the South Foreland Lighthouse (the first lighthouse in the world to use an electric light, and the site of Marconi's first international wireless transmission in 1899) to a series of viewpoints looking across to the French coast. On a clear day France is genuinely visible. The round trip takes around 2–3 hours at a comfortable pace. Wildflowers including orchids and scabious bloom in the chalk grassland in summer.

Free access · NT car park charge · Visitor centre has café
Safety note: Keep well back from the cliff edge. The chalk erodes and overhangs can break without warning. The National Trust path is clearly marked and safe; wandering near the edge is not.

Fan Bay Deep Shelter

A Second World War tunnel system hidden in the cliffs

Hidden in the cliffs near the visitor centre, Fan Bay Deep Shelter is a network of tunnels dug during the Second World War to house gun battery crews. The National Trust excavated and restored the tunnels — mostly intact but buried under decades of rubble — and opened them to visitors. Guided tours take small groups into the tunnel system, which includes dormitories, a magazine, and a gas decontamination room. It's an extraordinary survival of wartime infrastructure, and the contrast with the open landscape above is striking.

Guided tours ~£15 · Book in advance · 45 minutes

Dover Castle

England's most formidable coastal fortress

Overlooking the town and cliffs, Dover Castle has guarded the shortest Channel crossing since the Iron Age. The medieval keep built by Henry II in the 1180s is among the finest in England. The wartime tunnel network beneath the castle, used as the headquarters for Operation Dynamo (the Dunkirk evacuation) in 1940, adds a layer of 20th-century history that few medieval castles can match. English Heritage manages the site; allow at least a half-day for both the castle and the tunnels.

~£24 adults · English Heritage · Allow 3–4 hours
