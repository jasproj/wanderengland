The Tower of London & the Ravens

A thousand years of power, imprisonment, and a flock of birds that must never leave

The Tower of London is one of the most layered buildings in the world. Started by William the Conqueror in the early 1080s, it has served as a royal palace, a treasury, an armoury, a mint, a menagerie, a prison, and a place of execution — sometimes several of these at once. Today it holds the Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

And then there are the ravens. Eight of them live here, cared for by a senior Yeoman Warder called the Ravenmaster, their wings very slightly clipped to discourage flight. The legend says that if they ever leave, the Tower — and the kingdom — will fall.

The Raven Legend

A Victorian myth that became official policy

The most dramatic version of the legend traces to King Charles II: that an astronomer complained about ravens fouling his telescope, and Charles refused to remove them for fear of bad omens, relocating the Royal Observatory to Greenwich instead. The Tower's own historian has noted, however, that the earliest documented reference to captive ravens at the Tower appears to date from the Victorian era rather than the 17th century — suggesting the legend is more recent than it seems. Whatever its origins, the tradition is now rigorously maintained. The current Ravenmaster cares for around eight ravens, each named and with a distinct personality. They receive a daily diet of raw meat and are known to be fiercely territorial.

Ravenmaster is a senior Yeoman Warder post · Ravens can live 20+ years

The Crown Jewels

The real thing, still used at coronations

The Jewel House holds the Crown Jewels of the United Kingdom — a collection of royal regalia that includes the Imperial State Crown (set with over 2,800 diamonds, plus the Black Prince's Ruby and the Stuart Sapphire), the Sovereign's Orb, the Sovereign's Sceptre, and St Edward's Crown used at coronations. These are working objects of state, not museum replicas. A moving walkway carries visitors past the most famous pieces. The queues can be significant — go as early as possible or in the final hour before closing.

Included in Tower admission · Go first or last to avoid longest queues
Yeoman Warder tours: Free guided tours leave from the main gate approximately every 30 minutes. They last around an hour and cover the highlights with theatrical storytelling. Booking isn't needed — just join the group.

The White Tower

The original keep — Norman, stark, and still impressive

The White Tower is the original Norman keep at the heart of the complex, built in pale Caen stone imported from Normandy. Its four floors house the Royal Armouries' collection — armour worn by Henry VIII, Charles I, and other monarchs, alongside tournament armour, weapons, and the Line of Kings display. The Chapel of St John the Evangelist on the upper floor is one of the finest examples of Norman architecture in England, with its simple rounded arches and heavy piers unchanged since the 11th century.

Allow 45–60 min · Chapel of St John is a highlight

The Traitors' Gate and the Bloody Tower

Where prisoners arrived — and sometimes never left

The Traitors' Gate is the watergate through which prisoners arrived by boat from the River Thames, so called because it was the entry point for those accused of treason. Anne Boleyn, Thomas More, and Walter Raleigh all passed through it. The Bloody Tower above takes its name from the alleged murder of the young Princes in the Tower — the two sons of Edward IV who disappeared here in 1483, almost certainly on the orders of their uncle Richard III, though the full truth remains unresolved.
